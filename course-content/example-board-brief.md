# The decision

Should Aura increase acquisition spending next quarter, or first improve customer activation and transaction reliability? I recommend holding the broad increase while authorizing a bounded investigation and a separately costed acquisition test. The Board should approve the test size and exit conditions after reviewing the missing evidence.

# Evidence I checked

Revenue rose from MYR570,000 in May to MYR600,000 in June. Over the same period, CAC per verified customer rose from MYR54 to MYR66, while 30-day activation fell from 42% to 38%. June contribution margin was 36%, down from 40%; this excludes acquisition spending and fixed overhead. Source: finance workbook, Actuals, and customer CSV, May and June records.

June transaction success was 300,000 completed transactions divided by 310,000 attempts: 96.77%. May's rate was 98%. The mobile-acquired cohort had 35% activation and MYR70 CAC in June. These cohort figures do not establish which application surface caused the change. Source: service JSON, monthly_metrics; customer CSV; finance Actuals.

The growth deck's 52,000 login-active customers and the executive view's 40,000 transacting customers use different definitions. Neither should replace the other without its label. Source: growth review slides 2–3 and customer CSV, June login_active_customers and monthly_transacting_customers.

# Scenario and counterargument

A 20% increase in June's spending run rate adds MYR132,000 per month. Assuming CAC stays MYR66 and activation stays 38%, it implies about 2,000 additional verified customers and 760 additional activated customers. These are conditional calculations; they omit retention, capacity and lifetime value and establish no ROI.

The strongest counterargument is that growing revenue and a larger customer base could justify a controlled acquisition increase. Holding all growth activity could miss a valuable opportunity. A capped experiment with reliable measurements may therefore be preferable to an indefinite freeze.

# Owner and review condition

The CFO should verify cohort economics and intervention costs; the CTO should investigate failure patterns and distinguish device, provider and customer origin. Retention, fraud losses, lifetime value and the cost of improving activation remain missing. The CEO should return with those findings before the next spending approval. I would reconsider the increase when a costed test demonstrates acceptable activation and transaction success without worsening CAC. The June Board targets provide a discussion reference, not proof of future returns.
